# Translating references

A source can contribute an organizing principle, construction method, expressive decision, or relationship among parts. Ask what its material and audience make possible before moving the idea.

Useful observation names a relation you can point to: a quiet margin protects a functional opening; repeated syntax delays one decisive change; a sound continues across a cut; the same control retains its place while its state changes. An application names the need in the current brief. An adaptation names what you will actually construct and what has to change because the medium, audience, scale, or use differs.

Keep a compact working note when it helps:

- Source and inspected location, page, time range, or state.
- Observed property and its effect; distinguish your interpretation from documented intent.
- Application to the brief and specific adaptation in the artifact.
- Limitation, cultural context, rights, or lost affordance that affects that choice.

References should disagree usefully. One may show structure, another pacing or construction; they need not supply competing visual skins. If a collection dominates because it is easy to access, search the unresolved property in another source tradition. If all candidates are weak fits, change the query or discover elsewhere. Novelty alone is insufficient reason to add a foreign form.

When a reference feels pasted on, remove its surface identifiers and see whether its useful relation remains. If it disappears, identify a stronger transfer or drop it. For extensions to established work, test the adaptation against adjacent material before introducing a new identity.
